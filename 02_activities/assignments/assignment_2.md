    - Explain (with reference to material covered up to date, along with readings and other scholarly sources, as needed) why you classified that visualization the way you did.
      ```
      Visualization 1 - Good Visualization: Mortality attributable to dietary composition and weight. Global Nutrition Report. n.d. Available from: https://globalnutritionreport.org/resources/nutrition-profiles/north-america/northern-america/canada/. Note: this webpage contains many visualizations, the one I have selected is part way down the page, approximately figure/visualization #9.
      
        Aesthetic - I think visualization 1 is pleasing to look at because it is simple and clean. The bars are spaced a good distance apart, which makes it easy to read and I find that the colours used within each bar generally have good contrast, although I have good vision so this may not be the case for all viewers. The visualization also seems neutral, with no obvious opinions conveyed through the title or design choices (although I understand that complete neutrality is not possible). 

        Substantive - The visualization appears to accurately represent the data and does not seem to manipulate the design to push a specific message. The source of the data is included and appears credible.

        Perceptual - The visualization makes it easy to identify overall patterns, such as cancer and coronary heart disease being the leading causes of mortality across many risk factors. Within individual risk factors, viewers can also quickly identify the most common causes of death. For example, for a diet low in legumes, almost all deaths are attributable to coronary heart disease. The visualization is also well targeted to its intended audience, likely people working in nutrition and health, such as researchers, policymakers, and healthcare professionals, who will be familiar with the terminology used.
    
      Visualization 2 - Bad Visualization: pie-chart. sunita. 2015. Available from: https://public.tableau.com/app/profile/sunita6844/viz/pie-chart/pie-chart.

        Aesthetic - Although the colours are fun and bright, I do not think this visualization is nice to look at for its purpose of visualizing sales data by product sub-categories. Specifically, the large number of sub-categories (n=17) makes the pie chart feel overwhelming due to the many colours and similar colour variations (e.g., dark pink, light pink, light purple, light red, dark red). The colour palette also requires frequent reference to the legend, which is quite long and increases the time and cogncognitive effort needed to identify the correct subcategory. Therefore, the aesthetic design of the pie chart is not ideal as it requires added viewer effort to interpret the information.

        Substantive - This visualization does not provide a data source and lacks context about the underlying data. For example, it is not clear if the data represents sales of a specific company, industry or across a broader market. As a result, it is not possible to gauge if the data is being presented accurately or the significance of the data. Additionally, the intended usage of the visualization is not clear because we cannot tell if it was created for a report, infographic, presentation or other purpose. This information would help to understand if visualization is appropriate.

        Perceptual - The main message I take from this visualization is that phones and chairs have the highest sales. However, it is unclear whether sales are measured by the number of items sold, sales revenue, or another metric. As mentioned previously, there is also no context regarding who is selling the products or who is making the purchases. In addition, pie charts make it difficult to compare the sizes of slices accurately, particularly for smaller categories (e.g., envelopes versus labels) or categories with similar sales values (e.g., appliances versus bookcases).

      ```
    - How could this data visualization have been improved?  
      ```
      For visualization 1 (good), one minor improvement would be to increase the legend and axes label font size. Additionally, one limitation of stacked bar charts is that it can be difficult to compare the sizes of individual segments within bars. For example, it is hard to determine how many more cancer deaths are attributable to obesity compared with overweight, or whether cancer or coronary heart disease contributes more deaths overall. However, since the visualization appears on a general overview page about nutrition and health in Canada, its goal is likely to communicate broad relationships and patterns rather than exact quantities, which it does effectively.

      For visualization 2 (bad), currently, it is difficult to compare slices that are similar in size, making it challenging to identify meaningful differences between sub-categories. Although viewers can hover over individual slices to see the exact sales values, requiring this level of interaction suggests that the visualization may not be the most effective way to communicate the data. A bar graph with one bar per sub-category, ordered from highest to lowest sales, would likely be a more appropriate choice. This would make comparisons of sales between categories much easier and allow viewers to identify patterns more quickly. A bar graph would also eliminate the need for numerous colours and constant reference to the legend, as each bar could be labelled directly with its corresponding sub-category name.
      ```
